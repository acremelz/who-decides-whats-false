Мәселе

The Problem

According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs, by mid-September 2025, 52 people in Kazakhstan had been found responsible that year for spreading “false information”: one person under the Criminal Code and 51 people under the Administrative Code (24.kz, 2025). Factcheck.kz, the country’s first professional fact-checker, publishes about 1,200 checks a year in Kazakh and Russian, according to MediaNet, which runs it (MediaNet, n.d.). Kazakhstan does not just have a “fake news” problem, it has a verification gap: the definition of what is false is increasingly being defined by the government.

News has moved to platforms faster than checking has. In a 2026 phone survey of 1,100 people, 55.9% named social networks as their main news source, led by Instagram (58.0%) and TikTok (39.1%) (Demoscope, 2026). Most respondents do not trust the sources of the news they consume on social media pages (62.9%) and bloggers (66.1%), with state media being considered the most trusted source of information (57.4%). There is a second digital divide based on language, with 76.5% of people now consuming news at least partially in Kazakh (Demoscope, 2026). Among Russian speakers, views shifted pro-Russian after February 2022 as their use of Russian online news grew (Tkachenko et al., 2025).

There is also a regional divide, with the percentage of people who have Kazakh as their main home language ranging from 12% in Kostanay to 89% in Mangystau (Central Asia Barometer, 2023). In both languages, false information circulates on a small number of platforms, with the threat of legal action causing the most sensitive claims to migrate to private WhatsApp and Telegram groups where it is more challenging to verify (Filipov & Sulaimonova, 2026).

Four changes are widening the gap. In 2022, 77% of Demoscope respondents chose to answer in Russian; in 2026, 65.8% answered in Kazakh, a rough sign that the audience is moving toward Kazakh (NewTimes.kz, 2022; Demoscope, 2026). 2023 saw the introduction of the administrative “false information” offence in Kazakhstan and August 2025 saw the opening of the Center for Combating Disinformation by the government (Global Voices, 2024; Kazinform, 2025). Kazakhstan’s ranking in the RSF press freedom index dropped from 141 in 2025 to 149 in 2026 (Orda.kz, 2026). Automated tools developed for English do not work on Kazakh news: an English-trained model tested on machine-translated Kazakh news achieved only an F1 score of 0.40 (Telman et al., 2026).

The social-science question. When the state both prosecutes “false information” and runs its own fact-checking centre, who gets to decide what is false in Kazakhstan, and which language communities are left without independent verification?

The problem is not a shortage of technology. It sits in people, who rely on sources they distrust; in institutions, since independent fact-checking is “professionally recognized but institutionally fragile” (Filipov & Sulaimonova, 2026); and in power, because the authority to label a claim false is also the authority to punish it. It is hurting the regional Kazakh language audiences the most because although their portion of the information landscape is increasing, they lack the resources to fight misinformation the most. Journalists, who face criminal charges under Article 274 (CPJ, 2026), carry the legal risk.

Scope: false and misleading claims on social platforms in Kazakh and Russian, 2022–2026.

The state counts what it punishes

Enforcement figures are the most detailed official figures. In January–April 2023, 29 criminal investigations were registered for Article 274. One case made it to court, eight were dismissed as no crime was committed, eight were suspended due to the absence of suspects, and twelve were pending, according to a letter from the Prosecutor General to the Mazhilis deputies (Bizmedia, 2023). Three people were imprisoned under this article as of October 2023 (Tengrinews, 2023). The same pattern holds for 2025. 34 criminal cases were opened in January–May (Inbusiness.kz, 2025). In the first eight months of 2025, only one person was held liable under Article 274; by mid-September, 51 had been punished under the administrative Article 456-2. Of the 51, 48 were fined and three were arrested (24.kz, 2025).

The figure shows two things. Criminal cases rarely reach court, so the investigation itself becomes the pressure. And most punishment now runs through the administrative route: fines instead of criminal trials.

What the surveys show

Surveys reveal a gap between use and trust. 55.9% of respondents get their news mainly from social networks, but 62.9% do not trust social media pages and groups and 66.1% do not trust bloggers, while 57.4% trust state media (Demoscope, 2026). Surveys in 2023 indicate 38% followed social media in Russian, 18% in Kazakh, and 27% in both, while in 2026 45.4% consumed information mainly in Kazakh and only 21.1% in Russian alone (Demoscope, 2026; Internews & M-Vector, 2024). The two used different methods (face-to-face vs phone) and different questions (per channel vs overall), so they cannot be read as a trend. Both, however, show a large bilingual middle.

Where the data goes silent

  • Volume and language. No one measures how much false content circulates, or what share of it is in Kazakh.
  • Private chats. Sensitive claims move into WhatsApp and Telegram groups that neither monitors nor statistics can see (Filipov & Sulaimonova, 2026), and platform data is not public.
  • Outcomes. Of the 29 investigations in 2023, 20 were suspended or still open; we found no public record of how they ended.
  • Truth. Prosecution counts cannot separate real disinformation from criticism the state dislikes.
  • People outside the samples. The 2023 survey covered ages 14–65, so older people are missing.

How far can the numbers be trusted?

Enforcement numbers are official, but we receive them second-hand, through news reports about letters and briefings, and they are for different times and stages. One report mentions “about 40” in the lead paragraph and 34 in the main text (Inbusiness.kz, 2025). Demoscope’s survey is large and comprehensive for all regions, but it is part of a project by MediaNet, which also operates the fact-checking website Factcheck.kz (Lada.kz, 2026). The indices are expert opinions, which are helpful for direction, but not counting. The data is sufficient to inform about how the state enforces against “false information”, but not to determine how big a problem it is. The asymmetry of these things is the finding: the state counts what it punishes, not what is false.

Зерттеулер

Current State of Research

Research on disinformation in Kazakhstan comes from four camps that rarely cite each other. Read together, they reveal a contradiction that none of them sees alone.

Who is exposed

political behaviour

Tkachenko et al. (2025) combine six waves of the Central Asia Barometer, Yandex search data and an original 2023 online survey. They find that language habits steer media choice: Russian speakers moved toward pro-Russian views as their use of Russian online news grew, and Russian propaganda raises justification for evading sanctions. The design is careful, but the paper is an unreviewed preprint, and it studies foreign propaganda rather than domestic false claims or Kazakh-language content.

How fact-checking works

journalism studies

Filipov and Sulaimonova (2026) review 54 sources from 2017 to 2025. Legal risk pushes sensitive claims into private messaging, the Kazakh- and Russian-language spheres overlap without merging, and corrections reach far narrower audiences than the original claims. Tolegen et al. (2025) are more descriptive, combining bibliometrics with a 100-person poll, and advise readers to trust official sources. That advice assumes what is contested in Kazakhstan: that official sources are a neutral benchmark.

Who decides what is false

comparative politics and law

Globally, researchers treat “fake news” laws as tools of control. Lim and Bradshaw (2023) count 14 such laws worldwide in 2011–2015 and 91 in 2016–2022. Teo (2021), interviewing 17 journalists, academics and activists in Singapore, shows how such a law legitimises the state as the “ultimate fact-checker”. Dragomir (2025) compares countries and finds that governments often spread disinformation while writing laws against it. Applied to Kazakhstan, this literature predicts what Section 2 found: enforcement aimed at punishment rather than correction.

Can machines detect it

computational linguistics

A small research group has built the first Kazakh detection tools. Telman et al. (2026) show that English models fail on translated Kazakh (F1 0.40), while a model trained on 1,106 Kazakh texts scored F1 0.97, a result the authors call preliminary. Sambetbayeva et al. (2025) design an annotation scheme for over 5,000 Kazakh–Russian news texts, and Lamasheva et al. (2026) release KazFakeCorpus, with 4,276 texts. But the labels define truth by authority. Telman et al. take “true” texts only from portals registered with the Ministry of Information and Social Development, and KazFakeCorpus builds its real class from government news and its fake class with GPT-5.

What is known, contested and missing. It is known that language channels exposure and that “fake news” laws chill journalism. It is contested which language community is more at risk, and whether lab scores survive real social-media posts. What is missing is a link between the camps: the engineers build the state’s definition of truth into their training data, which is exactly what the political scientists warn against.

The gap. Researchers ask whether false claims can be detected in Kazakh, and who has the power to call them false. No one has asked how a Kazakh-language verification tool should be governed so that it strengthens independent checking instead of state labelling. That is the question this project’s solution takes up.

The AI-Assisted Solution, and What It Would Cost

The solution: a claim radar for independent fact-checkers

We propose an AI tool that helps Kazakhstan’s independent fact-checkers find and match false claims in Kazakh and Russian. It never labels content, scores people or removes anything. People decide what is false.

Why AI and not simply more people? Factcheck.kz publishes about 1,200 checks a year, while 15.7 million people in Kazakhstan use social media (MediaNet, n.d.; Kapital.kz, 2025). No team can read that much. Software can, as Full Fact’s jump from 100 to 100,000 claims a day shows (below).

What it does, in four steps:

  1. Collect. It reads public Telegram channels, news sites and video transcripts in both languages, plus claims that users forward to a tipline. The tipline reaches private WhatsApp and Telegram chats that public monitoring cannot see.
  2. Match. It groups repeated claims and links new posts to fact-checks already published, so each claim is checked once, not many times.
  3. Rank. It sorts claims by how widely they spread, so checkers start with the most-shared.
  4. Hand over. A fact-checker decides what is false, writes the check and posts it back on Instagram, WhatsApp and Telegram, where regional audiences already read news.
Illustrative

An example (illustrative, not a real case). A post claiming that a regional hospital has run out of insulin spreads in Kazakh. A reader forwards it to the tipline. The radar finds near-identical copies on several public Telegram channels, sees no existing check, and puts the claim at the top of the queue. A fact-checker calls the hospital and the regional health department and publishes the answer in both languages. From then on, the radar links every new copy to that check.

Built on
KazLLM, the open-source Kazakh model that Nazarbayev University’s ISSAI released in 2024, in its smaller 8-billion-parameter version (ISSAI, 2024). It can run on servers inside Kazakhstan. Why not a global chatbot such as ChatGPT? Messages would leave the country, which the data-localisation rule restricts, and English-trained tools fail in Kazakh (Telman et al., 2026).
Run by
A consortium of independent fact-checkers, such as Factcheck.kz, and a university research lab. Not the state’s Center for Combating Disinformation, because a state that punishes “false information” cannot also be the neutral judge of it.
Reaching
Kazakh-language audiences in the regions first, because their share of the information space is growing while the tools to check it are weakest.

Two case studies

UK

Full Fact AI (United Kingdom)

Full Fact built tools that monitor media, spot repeated claims and transcribe live broadcasts, funded by a $2 million, three-year Google.org grant won in 2019 (Friedrich Naumann Foundation, 2023). Detection rose from about 100 claims a day to 100,000 (Smalley, 2023), and the tools spread to more than 40 organisations in 30 countries (Full Fact, n.d.). In a 2023–2025 pilot with the Arab Fact-Checking Network, more than 25 organisations used them, and 13 of the 15 surveyed said they significantly sped up their work. Then Google funding cuts in 2025 cost the network its access (Wilkinson, 2026).

AR

Chequeabot (Argentina)

Chequeado’s bot detects checkable claims, matches repeats and transcribes speeches. Its Spanish tools had to be built mostly from scratch (Funke, 2018). Chequeado reports hundreds of hours saved for itself and seven other organisations, with transcription time down 90% (Sohr & Piccato, 2024).

What transfers, and what doesn’t. The division of labour transfers: machines find and match, people judge. The setting does not. Both results are self-reported, both come from democracies, and both depended on one large tech funder. A third case is a warning. Georgia’s MythDetector still reviews AI flags by hand because its tool “hasn’t been trained in the Georgian language” (Kahn, 2024). Kazakh faces the same problem, which is why the tool must run on a Kazakh model.

The bill: we fact-checked our own proposal

The bill covers five costs: environmental, economic, labour, political and governance, and data ethics. For each, we fact-checked the claim a promoter would make, using the verdict scale fact-checkers use.

Claim 1 Environmental“It barely uses any energy.”

Verdict: Mostly true

One server with one H100 graphics card, rated at 350 W maximum (NVIDIA, n.d.), draws about 0.7 kW in total if it runs all day. That is roughly 6,100 kWh a year, close to one Kazakhstani’s yearly electricity use of about 5,800 kWh (our estimate; LowCarbonPower, 2026). But coal produced 62% of Kazakhstan’s electricity in 2025 (Ember, 2026), and sources put the grid at 600–800 g of CO₂ per kWh (LowCarbonPower, 2026; Inzonex, n.d.). The server would emit about 4–5 tonnes of CO₂ a year, a quarter to a third of one person’s annual 14.9 tonnes (Our World in Data, n.d.). The 70B model would need two to four times the power, so the smaller model is the responsible choice. Water use is indirect, at the power plants; an air-cooled server needs none of its own. The heaviest compute, training KazLLM on eight NVIDIA DGX H100 nodes, has already been spent (ISSAI, n.d.).

Claim 2 Economic“The AI is free, because KazLLM is open source.”

Verdict: Misleading

The model is free to download, but the public has already paid for its Kazakh training data: the state allocated 3.9 billion tenge to collect it (Bizmedia, 2024). The running cost is people. Four full-time staff (an engineer, two annotators and an editor) at the 2025 national average wage of 443,315 tenge a month (BNS, 2026) cost about 21 million tenge a year before taxes (our estimate; an engineer would cost more). Who pays gets leverage: foreign donors, platforms such as Meta, whose programme Factcheck.kz joined in 2024 (MediaNet, 2024), or the state. Who profits? Platforms, which hand hard moderation calls to checkers, and Astana Hub, which holds the model. Full Fact shows the danger of one funder: when the money stopped, so did the tools.

Claim 3 Labour“AI will replace manual fact-checking.”

Verdict: False

It moves the work. The people who scroll channels all day lose most of that task; at Chequeado, one person now does a job that once took six (Sohr & Piccato, 2024). Verification grows instead, because the tool surfaces more claims than people can check. The tool also depends on hidden labour: annotators who label Kazakh training data (peer-reviewed corpora hold 1,106 to just over 5,000 texts), reviewers who read disturbing content, and engineers who maintain the model. That last job has no owner. ISSAI says it was not asked to develop KazLLM further after handing it to Astana Hub in December 2024 (ISSAI, 2026).

Claim 4 Political and governance“A fact-checking tool cannot be used for censorship.”

Verdict: False

In Kazakhstan, a “false” label can lead to punishment: 51 people were held liable under the administrative “false information” article in 2025, as of mid-September (24.kz, 2025). A list of viral false claims, and of the channels spreading them, is exactly what prosecutors would want.

The law makes the risk concrete. Personal data must be stored on servers in Kazakhstan (GSE, n.d.), within reach of the authorities. The Law on Artificial Intelligence, in force since 18 January 2026, sorts systems into minimal, medium and high risk and bans AI that creates or spreads prohibited content (EY, 2025; Tengrinews, 2026). Whoever decides what is “prohibited” decides what the tool may flag.

Control also matters at the model level. Astana Hub received KazLLM under a non-exclusive licence (ISSAI, 2026), so the consortium can run its own copy, but no independent body currently maintains the model. Buribayeva et al. (2026) find that Kazakhstan’s AI Law declares responsible-AI principles but leaves audit, contestation and independent oversight weakly specified, while one ministry acts as regulator, infrastructure operator and AI promoter. A tool built inside that structure would inherit its conflict of interest. Roberts et al. (2024) judge governance by its legitimacy: does it help actors coordinate, and do its decisions follow democratic procedures? A claim radar run by a ministry would fail the second test.

Claim 5 Data ethics“It only uses public data.”

Verdict: Missing context

Public channels are public, but their authors never agreed to be monitored, and tipline messages carry phone numbers and screenshots of private chats. A false flag could expose a person to criminal risk, and a leaked tipline log would show who forwarded which rumours, in a country that prosecutes spreading “false information”. And a model that learns from official sources inherits the “official = true” bias that Section 3 found in existing Kazakh corpora. Buribayeva et al. (2026) warn that sovereign models such as KazLLM, trained largely on national websites and official documents, may quietly privilege official interpretations. Our tool runs on that model, so honest labels are not optional.

✓

Қорытынды

Verdict: a conditional yes

Knowing these costs, Kazakhstan should build this tool only under five conditions:

  1. Independent control. Independent fact-checkers, a university and representatives of regional Kazakh-language audiences run it; no state body holds the keys or the data.
  2. People decide. The tool never labels or removes anything, and its outputs are never shared with law enforcement.
  3. Data minimisation. Tipline senders stay anonymous, private messages are deleted after checking, and the tool stores claims, not names.
  4. Honest labels. Training data comes from independent fact-checks with a published labelling method, not from official sources alone.
  5. Secure funding. Several years of funding from more than one source are in place before launch.

Each condition answers a line of the bill (Figure 2). Claims 1 and 3 do not block the project: the energy cost is modest, and the shift in labour can be planned.

Why a community seat. Mhlambi (2020) traces AI harms to excluding affected communities and concentrating power, and argues, from the ubuntu idea that a person is a person through other persons, that communities should control the tools used on them. The audiences whose claims get checked belong on the board, not only in the data.

How we would know it works. An independent evaluation, planned before launch, would track three numbers: how long a viral claim takes to get a check, what share of checks appear in Kazakh, and how many reach regional audiences. If the state ever requests the tool’s data, the evaluation should say so publicly.

If these conditions hold, Kazakh-language audiences get faster, independent checks where they actually read news. If they cannot be met, the same tool becomes a map of who says what, handed to a state that prosecutes “false information”. Then the honest answer is no.

A fact-checking machine is only as independent as the people who can switch it off.
